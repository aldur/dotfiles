# Writing Well

Follow the requested format, tone, scope, and project style rules. Preserve
facts, qualifications, citations, and the author's intent. For a review-only
request, report findings without edits. Use the brief and existing text to
identify what the reader needs to understand or do.

## Shape the piece

- Choose a narrow subject and one main point. Keep background that helps
  readers understand that point.
- Match the opening to the task. Lead a report or message with its finding
  or requested action. Open a narrative with a detail that creates interest.
- Put ideas in the order readers need them. Explain prerequisites before
  conclusions. Signal changes in time, viewpoint, or direction.
- Keep a consistent voice. Use the author's natural language; avoid added
  formality or forced familiarity.

## Revise

- Fix structure before sentences. Give each paragraph one connected purpose.
- Prefer familiar words, specific nouns, and exact verbs. Use active voice
  when the actor matters. Do not invent an actor to remove passive voice.
- Cut repetition and empty modifiers. Keep useful detail, necessary limits,
  and uncertainty. Use the same term for the same technical concept.
- Read consecutive sentences together. Fix unclear references and missing
  connections. Vary sentence length without breaking the flow of thought.
- Stop when the purpose is complete. Keep a closing summary when the format
  or reader needs it.

## Examples

These examples are original. They illustrate editing decisions, not facts
to add to a user's text. Apply a change only when the source supports it.

### Put the useful information first

Before:

> I am writing to let you know that, following our discussion of the release
> schedule, we have decided to move the release from Tuesday to Thursday.
> Please send any test failures by Wednesday.

After:

> The release has moved from Tuesday to Thursday. Please send test failures
> by Wednesday.

The new opening gives the decision directly. It keeps both dates and the
requested action.

### Replace abstract nouns with actions

Before:

> Implementation of the cache was performed by the service team for the
> purpose of reducing repeated reads.

After:

> The service team added a cache to reduce repeated reads.

The actor and action are clear. The sentence still states a purpose; it does
not claim that measurements proved an improvement.

### Supply a missing connection

The source explains that each worker reads a file and then replaces it with
its own copy.

Before:

> Two workers may update the same file. Updates may be lost.

After:

> When two workers update the same file, each reads it and then replaces it
> with its own copy. If both read before either writes, the second write can
> discard the first worker's changes.

The revision explains how the loss can occur. If the source did not describe
this process, flag the gap instead of supplying an assumed explanation.

### Keep uncertainty that changes the claim

Before:

> It would appear that the update may reduce battery use, based on a trial
> involving 12 devices.

After:

> In a trial of 12 devices, the update appeared to reduce battery use.

Avoid:

> The update reduces battery use.

The revision removes repeated caution but keeps the sample size and the
uncertain result. The last version claims more than the evidence supports.

### Keep detail that gives the writing character

Before:

> I was very nervous as I waited for the interview to begin. I kept folding
> the visitor badge until it split.

After:

> Waiting for the interview, I folded the visitor badge until it split.

The action shows the feeling and keeps the narrator's voice. A shorter
version such as "I waited nervously" would lose the useful detail.

### End with the finding

Before:

> The pilot met the latency target, but exceeded the cost limit. In conclusion,
> the pilot had both positive and negative results.

After:

> The pilot met the latency target but exceeded the cost limit.

The specific result is a stronger ending than a general restatement.

## Deliver

Return the requested draft, edit, or review. Flag missing evidence instead of
inventing facts, quotations, or personal experiences. Explain material changes
when useful; do not append an editing report unless requested.
